## The Issue We Are Addressing

Modern-day psychiatric care for obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) still relies heavily upon systems of trial and error. Consider a patient diagnosed with OCD who is prescribed a pill; that same patient must then wait 6 to 8 weeks to see if the pill works. If the pill causes serious side effects or fails to take any effect whatsoever, the patient needs to repeat the process with a different medication. Finding a medication that is suitable for a particular patient typically takes several months, if not years; in the meantime, it imposes emotional and financial damage. The process of prescribing specific SSRIs, SNRIs, and TCAs rarely considers how a patient’s personal biochemistry and diet may be affecting the medication. Within pharmaceutical companies, drugs are tested under ideal, isolated environments, not accounting for minerals within the body, such as magnesium (commonly taken as a supplement for sleep or anxiety). These limitations demonstrate the need for a more individualized approach to drug modeling — one that considers the biochemical variability between individuals (such as mineral complexation).

## Project Goal and Research Question

Building upon this idea, our project investigates how magnesium-induced complexation can affect the structural properties of OCD medications and how those changes limit their ability to cross the blood-brain barrier. Through a combination of computational modeling and UV-Vis spectroscopy, we aim to both clarify whether common ions like magnesium cause a difference in the efficacy of the drugs and create mathematical models that can improve psychiatrists’ ability to predict drug effectiveness for a given individual based on mineral levels.

To guide our investigation, we formulated the following research question: To what extent does magnesium‑induced complexation modify the geometry and electronic properties of SSRIs, SNRIs, and TCAs, and how do these computationally and spectroscopically observed changes affect their predicted ability to permeate the blood–brain barrier?

## Magnesium and Systemic Variability

Because magnesium levels fluctuate widely between different individuals, it is important to understand its systemic variability before modeling its interactions with different drug molecules. Magnesium levels in the human body vary depending on an individual’s diet and internal regulation; dietary intake of the mineral from foods and supplements raises serum Mg levels, while stress, medication use, and a faster metabolic rate lower them. The variability of [Mg²⁺] among individuals affects how much Mg is available to form complexes with various drugs, possibly related to molecular permeability across the blood-brain barrier. In the human body, magnesium is not a static mineral; it is constantly being regulated by our gastrointestinal systems. The localized pH gradients within the gastrointestinal tract can alter the ionization states of both the mineral and the medication. By acknowledging this, these experiments only confirm possible coordination complexes that happen in vitro (in a lab), and cannot necessarily be applied to the human body.

## Hypothesis

If magnesium chloride (MgCl₂) is introduced to over-the-counter drug analogs meant to mimic OCD medications like SSRIs, SNRIs, and TCAs, then the wet lab UV-VIs spectra will demonstrate a significant spectral shift indicating the creation of a coordinate complex. Computational modeling will show this interaction through measurable changes of lipophilicity (lower LogP) and reduced ability to cross lipid membranes (blood–brain barrier), together predicting the changed permeability across lipid membranes such as the blood-brain barrier.

## Analog Selection and Justification

To test this hypothesis within the constraints of a high school laboratory, we decided to use over-the-counter analogs that mimic the chemical properties of prescription OCD medications (containing a similar molecular structure, providing the same therapeutic effect, etc). To choose the analogs of the medications we intended to study, we needed to observe the structure of each drug and the key parts it needed to include if we wanted to use it in a wet lab with Mg²⁺ ions. There are three distinct categories of medications used to treat OCD: Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs), Serotonin and Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs), and Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs). For the analog of an SSRI, we decided to go with Diphenhydramine (the active ingredient in Benadryl). Diphenhydramine's chemical structure features a central carbon atom attached to two aromatic rings (a flat, ring-shaped molecule where electrons are shared evenly across all atoms), with a flexible carbon chain that mimics the structure of SSRIs like Fluoxetine. For SNRIs, our pick was Phenylephrine (an OTC nasal decongestant). Usually more polar than SSRIs, SNRIs often contain hydroxyl groups (−OH). Phenylephrine contains two of those hydroxyl groups, one in an aromatic ring and one in a chain, creating two competing areas of oxygen donors, which Mg²⁺ has a strong affinity for. Finally, for TCAs, we went with the antihistamine Chlorpheniramine. TCAs are named after their three-ring hydrophobic structure, and Chlorpheniramine mimics this with two rings bound to a carbon. It is also highly lipophilic, just like TCAs.
